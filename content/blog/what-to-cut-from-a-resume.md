If your resume is too long, cut the weakest evidence before you touch the formatting.

Do not start by shrinking the font, squeezing the margins, or turning every bullet into a dense sentence. Start by deciding what deserves space.

A resume is not a complete work history. It is a selected case for the job in front of you. Harvard's career guidance puts it plainly: a resume should summarize your abilities, education, and experience while highlighting your strongest assets for the reader's needs.[^harvard-resume]

Use this order:

1. Cut what does not help the target role.
2. Compress what is useful but old or repetitive.
3. Rewrite bullets that contain real proof.
4. Fix formatting last.

Tiny CV is useful here because markdown makes the tradeoff visible. If one bullet pushes the resume past a clean page, you can decide whether the evidence is worth keeping instead of hiding the problem inside a template.

## The resume cut decision tree

Use this decision tree on every line of the resume: if the line does not prove fit for the target job, remove it, compress it, or move it lower.

Work from the top down. Do not protect a section just because it has always been there.

**IF the line is unrelated to the target role, THEN cut it.**

This is usually the easiest deletion. A customer-support resume does not need a long bullet about redesigning a club poster unless the role values design, communications, or community work.

MIT Career Advising & Professional Development recommends building a resume for a specific position and selecting the experiences that demonstrate the skills required for that position.[^mit-resumes] That is the whole game.

**IF the line repeats a stronger point, THEN merge it or cut it.**

Two bullets that both say "managed schedules" are not twice as convincing. Keep the one with scale, stakes, tools, or outcome.

**IF the line is old but still credible, THEN compress it.**

Older roles can still matter. They may show domain depth, progression, or a recognizable employer. But they usually do not need four bullets.

Move them into an "Earlier Experience" or "Additional Experience" section with one strong proof point, or list only company, title, and dates.

**IF the line proves a target skill with evidence, THEN keep it.**

Evidence means action plus context. It can be a metric, a tool, a customer, a team size, a shipped project, a compliance constraint, or a before-and-after result.

**IF the line is a task with no outcome, THEN rewrite it only if the task matters.**

"Responsible for weekly reporting" is weak. "Built weekly revenue reporting in Google Sheets for a 12-person sales team" is stronger. If the reporting does not matter for the target role, cut it anyway.

## What to cut first

Cut the lines that make the reader work harder without making you look more qualified.

Start with the easy removals:

- Objective statements that only say you want the job.
- "References available upon request."
- Full street addresses.
- Personal details such as marital status, age, religion, or a photo.
- Unrelated hobbies.
- Coursework that no longer matters once you have stronger work or project evidence.
- Skills that are already proven in better bullets.
- Bullets that describe basic duties with no scope, tool, outcome, or relevance.

Yale's Office of Career Strategy lists common resume errors that include passive language, full-sentence narrative style, personal information, visual clutter, and listing all positions instead of selecting relevant ones.[^yale-formatting]

That last point is the key. A long resume often feels long because it refuses to choose.

Here is what this looks like in practice.

A retail worker applying to an operations coordinator role can cut "Assisted customers on sales floor" if the resume already shows stronger customer work. But they should keep "Reconciled closing cash drawer and flagged inventory mismatches across 400+ SKUs" if the target role values process, accuracy, and operations.

A software engineer can cut a one-line college project in Python if they now have production backend work. But they should keep the project if it is the only proof of machine learning, data pipelines, or another target skill.

A new graduate can cut a long course list after adding internships, labs, capstone projects, or leadership examples that show the same skills with more proof.

The question is not "Was this part of my life?" The question is "Does this help this reader say yes?"

## What to compress instead of cutting

Compress useful material when it gives context but no longer deserves prime space.

This is where many resumes get better quickly. You do not have to erase older work. You just have to stop giving it the same weight as recent, relevant proof.

Use compression for:

- Older jobs in the same field.
- Early roles that explain your path but not your current level.
- Repeated skills across several roles.
- Internships after you have full-time experience.
- Projects that show range but are not central to the target job.

CareerOneStop, sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor, recommends keeping resumes clean, easy to read, and typically within 1-2 pages while ordering sections to your advantage.[^careeronestop-resumes] That does not mean every job gets equal space.

Try this pattern:

**Before:**

- Customer Service Associate, BrightMart, 2018-2020
- Handled customer questions and returns
- Stocked shelves and organized inventory
- Trained new cashiers
- Helped with weekly schedules

**After:**

- Customer Service Associate, BrightMart, 2018-2020
- Trained 6 new cashiers and helped reconcile weekly inventory issues across front-end operations.

The older role still says something useful. It no longer steals the page.

Tiny CV's plain markdown source makes this kind of compression easier because you can collapse a section without fighting a drag-and-drop layout. Shorten the evidence first, then preview the page.

## How to trim resume bullets without losing proof

Trim resume bullets by removing setup words and keeping only the action, scope, tool, and result.

Weak bullets usually carry too much scaffolding:

- "Responsible for..."
- "Worked on..."
- "Helped with..."
- "Assisted in the development of..."
- "Was involved in..."

Yale recommends writing accomplishment statements with an Action + Project + Result structure.[^yale-bullets] MIT gives similar advice: start with a strong action verb, explain the relevant task, and extend it to an outcome.[^mit-skills]

That does not mean every bullet needs a dramatic metric. It means every bullet should give the reader something concrete.

**Before:** Responsible for managing social media accounts.

**After:** Scheduled weekly LinkedIn and Instagram posts for a 3-person marketing team, keeping campaigns on calendar during product launches.

**Before:** Worked on customer onboarding.

**After:** Rewrote onboarding email templates to clarify setup steps and reduce repeated support questions.

**Before:** Assisted with data analysis for sales team.

**After:** Cleaned Salesforce export data and built weekly pipeline summaries for account executives.

The shorter versions are not just shorter. They are clearer.

When editing in Tiny CV, treat every bullet like a line of markdown that has to earn its place. If the line only says you were present, cut it. If it shows judgment, ownership, or impact, sharpen it.

## How to make a resume one page

To make a resume one page, cut content first, compress older experience second, trim bullets third, and adjust formatting only after the writing is tight.

Here is the order that usually works:

1. Remove unrelated sections.
2. Cut duplicated skills.
3. Reduce older roles to one bullet or no bullets.
4. Keep 2-4 bullets for the most relevant recent roles.
5. Rewrite long bullets into one-line proof points where possible.
6. Then adjust spacing, font size, and margins within readable limits.

Yale's formatting guidance says each work experience should generally include no more than 3-4 bullets, and each bullet should be no more than 1-2 lines.[^yale-formatting] That is a useful constraint. If a role has six bullets, at least two of them should be suspicious.

Avoid fake one-page fixes:

- Do not use tiny type.
- Do not remove all white space.
- Do not turn bullets into paragraphs.
- Do not hide important dates.
- Do not use design tricks that make the PDF harder to scan.

A clean one-page resume should feel edited, not squeezed.

If you want to rebuild after cutting, Tiny CV is designed around markdown source, paper preview, PDF export, templates, and public CV links. The point is not to make the page decorative. The point is to make the strongest version easy to maintain.

## What to keep even when space is tight

Keep the strongest proof for the target role, even if that means cutting a section you personally like.

Good resume editing is not minimalism for its own sake. It is prioritization.

Keep:

- Recent roles that match the target job.
- Promotions or level changes.
- Recognizable employers, clients, products, or domains when relevant.
- Certifications or licenses required for the work.
- Tools and technical skills you can actually use.
- Results with real scope.
- Projects that prove a skill the job description asks for.

NACE defines career readiness through eight competencies: career and self-development, communication, critical thinking, equity and inclusion, leadership, professionalism, teamwork, and technology.[^nace-competencies] You do not need to name those competencies on the resume. But they are a useful filter.

If a bullet proves communication, critical thinking, leadership, teamwork, or technology in the context of the target role, think twice before cutting it.

For role-specific language, O*NET OnLine is a better source than guessing. It provides occupation profiles with tasks, skills, work activities, and technology requirements across 900+ occupations.[^onet-online] Use it to understand what the job actually values, then keep the resume lines that map to that work.

This also keeps you away from bad ATS folklore.

Do not stuff keywords. Do not add skills you cannot defend. Do not keep a bloated skills section because someone told you "the ATS needs it." Keep accurate terms where they naturally describe your real experience.

If you are tailoring for a specific job, use the same discipline: move relevant proof forward, cut what does not help, and keep the facts stable. Shortening and tailoring are not separate tasks. They are both ways of making the resume choose.

## A quick before-and-after walkthrough

The fastest way to shorten an overlong resume is to label each line before rewriting it.

Here is an overlong entry for someone moving from general operations into customer success.

**Before:**

Customer Operations Associate, Northstar Supply

- Responded to customer emails and phone calls.
- Worked with the sales team on customer questions.
- Helped maintain customer records in HubSpot.
- Created weekly reports for managers.
- Assisted with onboarding new customers.
- Participated in team meetings and shared updates.

Now label it:

- Responded to customer emails and phone calls. **Compress.** Basic duty, but customer context matters.
- Worked with the sales team on customer questions. **Merge.** Useful only if tied to escalation or retention.
- Helped maintain customer records in HubSpot. **Keep if the target role uses CRM. Rewrite.**
- Created weekly reports for managers. **Keep if reporting mattered. Rewrite.**
- Assisted with onboarding new customers. **Keep. Rewrite with scope.**
- Participated in team meetings and shared updates. **Cut.** Too generic.

**After:**

Customer Operations Associate, Northstar Supply

- Managed customer email and phone support while maintaining account records in HubSpot.
- Coordinated sales escalations and onboarding follow-ups for new customer accounts.
- Built weekly customer issue summaries for managers to spot recurring account risks.

This version is shorter. It is also more useful.

It keeps customer communication, CRM fluency, sales coordination, onboarding, and reporting. It cuts the line that only says the candidate attended meetings.

That is the standard.

## Final checklist: cut, compress, then format

Use this checklist when your resume is too long and you need a clean final pass.

| If the line... | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| Does not support the target role | Cut it |
| Repeats a stronger line | Merge or cut it |
| Is old but still useful | Compress it |
| Shows a required skill with evidence | Keep it |
| Describes a duty with no outcome | Rewrite it or cut it |
| Exists only because a template asked for it | Question it |
| Makes the page fit only by shrinking readability | Fix the content instead |

The best resume cut is the one the reader never misses.

Tiny CV can help you keep that editing loop clean: write in markdown, preview the page, export a PDF, and publish a focused CV link when the story is tight. But the real decision still happens line by line.

Keep the proof. Cut the rest.
