Send the PDF when a system asks for your resume. Send the public link when a person needs to read, forward, or revisit it quickly.

That is the whole decision.

A PDF is the formal artifact. It preserves the page, fits job applications, and gives recruiters something they can save in a candidate record. A public resume link is the lightweight version. It works better in outreach, warm intros, LinkedIn messages, mobile review, and follow-ups.

The cleanest workflow is not choosing one forever. It is knowing which one leads in each context.

Tiny CV is built around that split: write the resume once in markdown, publish a clean public CV link, and export a PDF when the hiring process asks for a document.

## The sending matrix

Use this matrix when you are deciding whether to send a resume link, a PDF, or both.

| Situation | Send first | Add this too | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Job portal or ATS upload | PDF | Nothing unless requested | The employer asked for a file, so follow the process. |
| Recruiter email | PDF | Public resume link | The PDF is formal; the link is faster to open and forward. |
| Warm intro | Public resume link | PDF if requested | The person forwarding you needs a clean, low-friction asset. |
| LinkedIn outreach | Public resume link | Offer the PDF | A link fits the channel better than an attachment. |
| Hiring manager follow-up | Public resume link | PDF if they need it | The thread stays clean and the resume is easy to reopen. |
| Application that asks for a specific format | That exact format | Nothing extra unless there is room | Instructions beat personal preference. |

Career offices generally treat resume materials as formal application documents, which is why PDF is still the safe default for applications.[^1] But outreach is not the same as an application. In outreach, the reader is usually deciding whether to take the next step, not archiving your file yet.

That is where a public link helps.

## Decision tree: resume link or PDF?

If the employer gives a specific instruction, follow the instruction.

Do not fight the form. If the posting says upload a PDF, upload a PDF. If it asks for a Word document, send that. If it asks you to paste plain text, paste plain text. The strongest format is the one the receiver can actually process.

**If the resume is going into a job portal, use a PDF.**

Most formal applications are built around uploaded documents. A PDF keeps your one-page layout stable and gives the hiring team a predictable file. MIT Career Advising and Professional Development, Harvard career services, UC Davis, and CareerOneStop all frame resumes as structured documents that should be clear, concise, and easy to review.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

**If a person is reading your message, include a public link.**

Recruiter email, warm intro, LinkedIn DM, community referral, founder note, and hiring-manager follow-up are human channels. The reader may be on a phone. They may forward your note internally. They may want to skim before saving anything.

A link is easier in that moment.

**If you are emailing a recruiter, usually send both.**

Attach the PDF, then add one sentence with the link:

> I attached my resume as a PDF, and here is a public version if that is easier to open or forward: [your link].

That sentence does not over-explain. It gives the recruiter options.

**If you are sending a warm intro, lead with the link.**

The person introducing you is doing you a favor. Do not make them manage attachments, version names, or long context. Give them one clean link and a one-line description of what you are looking for.

**If privacy is a concern, do not publish your most personal resume.**

A public CV link should not include your full home address, private references, personal identification details, or anything you would not want forwarded. UC Davis career guidance recommends city and state rather than a full address, excludes sensitive personal details, and keeps references off the resume until an employer asks.[^4]

## Where PDFs still win

PDFs still win when the resume needs to behave like a formal document.

That includes job applications, recruiter records, interview packets, printouts, and any process where the recipient expects a file. A PDF is also useful when the resume has to preserve exact page breaks, spacing, and layout.

The practical reason is boring, which is why it matters: hiring workflows are built around documents.

A public link can be beautiful and fast, but it should not replace the file an employer asked for. If a portal has a required upload field, your public resume link is a supplement at most. It is not the submission.

This is also why a resume builder should produce both a focused page and a reliable export. The page helps people read. The export helps systems accept.

Tiny CV keeps those connected. The markdown source stays readable, the public page stays clean, and the PDF export is there when the process turns formal.

## Where public resume links win

Public resume links win when the resume needs to move through people.

Think about the path of a warm referral. Someone meets you, likes your work, and wants to send your background to a hiring manager. A link is easier to forward than a file called `Andrew_Resume_Final_v7.pdf`.

The same logic applies to LinkedIn outreach. LinkedIn's own help center treats public profile URLs as shareable links you can copy into messages, which is the same low-friction behavior you want from a public CV link.[^5] A short note with a clean resume link feels native to the channel. A file attachment can feel heavier than the relationship.

Links also work better for quick mobile review. The W3C’s accessibility guidance emphasizes clear link text and readable web content because people scan and navigate web pages in uneven conditions.[^6] Your resume is no different. If someone opens it between meetings, the public version should load fast and make the strongest evidence easy to find.

There is another benefit: version control.

If you send a PDF, that file is frozen. If you share a canonical public resume link, you can fix a typo, tighten a summary, or update a project without asking everyone to delete the old file. That does not mean you should rewrite your story constantly. It means your public version can stay current.

Tiny CV fits this use case because the hosted link is not a portfolio homepage pretending to be a resume. It is a resume: one page, readable, shareable, and backed by markdown.

## Email and outreach examples

The best resume-sharing line is short enough that nobody notices it.

Use these patterns.

**Recruiter email**

> I attached my resume as a PDF. I also keep a public version here if that is easier to open or forward: [link].

**Warm intro**

> Here is my one-page CV link for context: [link]. I can send a PDF if useful.

**LinkedIn outreach**

> I am exploring product engineering roles at small teams. Here is my public CV: [link].

**Follow-up after a call**

> Resurfacing my resume link here so it is easy to find from the thread: [link].

These lines work because they do not ask the reader to learn your resume workflow. They just make the next action easy.

## Privacy and version control rules

A public resume should be intentionally public, not accidentally over-shared.

Use a version that is safe to forward. City and state are usually enough if location matters. A professional email address is fine. A full street address, private references, sensitive immigration details, compensation history, or personal identification details should stay out unless a specific formal process requires them.

Keep role-specific claims in role-specific versions. If you are applying to a data role, the PDF can emphasize analytics work. If you are applying to a product role, the PDF can emphasize customer-facing launches. The public link should be broad enough to represent you well across normal sharing contexts.

The same rule applies when you tailor a resume for a role: change the emphasis, not the facts.

Tiny CV makes this cleaner if you maintain one canonical markdown source, publish a general public resume link, and export targeted PDFs when the application deserves a tailored version.

## The final rule

Default to both when the channel supports it, but lead with the format that matches the reader.

If a system is asking, lead with the PDF.

If a person is deciding whether to talk to you, lead with the link.

If you are emailing a recruiter, attach the PDF and include the public link in one quiet sentence.

If you are sending a LinkedIn note, warm intro, or follow-up, the link usually does the job better.

The goal is not to look more modern. The goal is to remove friction without ignoring the hiring process in front of you.
