Tailor your resume by changing the order, emphasis, and wording of true experience, not by copying the job description into your document.

That is the difference between a focused resume and keyword stuffing.

A tailored resume should make the target role obvious in the first screenful. It should use the employer's language where it accurately describes your work, move the most relevant proof higher, and remove details that distract from the role.

This protocol takes about 30 minutes for one job description. Use it when you already have a baseline resume and need a stronger version for a specific application.

Tiny CV is useful for this because markdown makes tailoring visible. You can duplicate a version, edit the bullets directly, preview whether the one-page resume still fits, and export a PDF only after the evidence is tight.

## The rule: match the job, do not mimic the job post

Resume tailoring means making your real experience easier to match to the employer's stated needs.

It does not mean inventing projects, inflating titles, or repeating phrases until the resume sounds like a scraped job ad.

Harvard's Mignone Center says a resume should reflect the skills, experience, and impact a specific employer would value, while also staying clear, direct, fact-based, and easy to scan.[^harvard] MIT's career office gives the same practical structure for bullets: start with a strong action verb, add the relevant task or context, reflect appropriate job-description language, and extend it to an outcome.[^mit]

The key word is appropriate.

If the job post says "customer onboarding" and your resume says "new client setup," you can probably use the employer's phrase. If the job post says "enterprise Salesforce administration" and you only updated a small spreadsheet, you cannot borrow that phrase without misleading the reader.

Use this test:

| If the job description says... | Ask yourself... | Resume move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A skill you have used | Where did I use it? | Add the term to the relevant bullet or skills line. |
| A responsibility you owned | What project proves it? | Move that project higher. |
| A tool you used lightly | Can I explain my level? | Mention it honestly, with scope. |
| A qualification you do not have | Is there adjacent proof? | Show the adjacent proof, but do not claim the missing skill. |
| A buzzword with no evidence | What would a manager confirm? | Replace the buzzword with concrete work. |

This keeps the resume aligned without turning it into a keyword dump.

## Step 1: Pull out the real requirements

Start by separating required qualifications from nice-to-have details and repeated filler.

Most job descriptions mix signals. Some lines describe the job. Some describe the company. Some are legal boilerplate. Some are a wish list. Your job is to find the few things the reviewer will actually scan for.

Copy the job description into a scratch note and mark four groups:

1. Required skills or experience.
2. Recurring verbs, such as "build," "coordinate," "analyze," "sell," "support," or "ship."
3. Tools, certifications, domains, or customer types.
4. Outcomes the role is supposed to create.

Do not rewrite the resume yet.

CareerOneStop, sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor, recommends making it easy for employers to find the qualifications and skills that relate to the position, and it specifically advises using keywords from the posting for required or desired skills and experience.[^careeronestop]

That is the useful version of keyword advice. You are not trying to match every noun. You are trying to identify the language that accurately names the work.

For example, a product engineering job might repeat:

- Build user-facing features.
- Work with product and design.
- Own ambiguous projects.
- Debug production issues.
- Use React, TypeScript, and SQL.
- Improve onboarding or activation.

That gives you a tailoring map. It does not give you permission to add every phrase to your resume.

Checkpoint: after this step, you should have 6-10 target signals, not a highlighted wall of text.

## Step 2: Build a proof inventory before editing

Before you touch the resume, match each target signal to evidence you can defend.

Make a two-column note:

| Target signal | My proof |
| --- | --- |
| React and TypeScript | Built account settings flow in React; refactored form validation in TypeScript. |
| Product/design collaboration | Worked with PM and designer on checkout experiment. |
| SQL | Wrote weekly activation queries; not a database owner. |
| Ambiguous projects | Owned support-to-engineering bug triage when no process existed. |
| Onboarding | Rebuilt setup checklist and lifecycle emails. |

This step prevents the most common tailoring mistake: editing toward the job description before you know what evidence you actually have.

NACE's career readiness framework is helpful here because employers are not only looking for tool names. NACE lists eight competencies, including communication, critical thinking, leadership, teamwork, professionalism, and technology.[^nace] A good tailored resume often proves several of those at once.

If the job asks for "cross-functional collaboration," the proof might be a launch, escalation process, customer handoff, design review, or operations project. If the job asks for "analytical judgment," the proof might be a dashboard, audit, experiment, forecast, or decision memo.

Tiny CV's markdown workflow makes this easier if you keep private notes under the role while drafting. Write the proof inventory, turn the best lines into bullets, then delete the scratch notes before publishing or exporting.

Checkpoint: every target signal you plan to add should have a real project, responsibility, tool, or outcome behind it.

## Step 3: Reorder the resume around the target role

The fastest tailoring win is moving the strongest relevant evidence higher.

A hiring manager should not have to read the whole page to discover that you fit the role. If the job is about customer success, the strongest customer-facing proof should appear near the top. If the job is about backend systems, infrastructure and data work should not be buried under a generic project.

Use this order of operations:

1. Rewrite the headline or summary only if it helps the target role.
2. Move the most relevant skills to the front of the skills section.
3. Reorder bullets inside each role so the best match appears first.
4. Compress or cut bullets that do not support the target role.
5. Move older but relevant projects above newer but weaker filler only when that helps the reader.

This is where tailoring connects to one-page editing. If the page gets too long, do not squeeze the layout first. Cut weaker evidence. The same deletion-first rule applies in [what to cut from a resume when it is too long](/blog/what-to-cut-from-a-resume).

The U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET system is a good outside reference when a job title is fuzzy. O*NET describes occupations by knowledge, skills, abilities, tasks, work activities, and other descriptors, and the Department of Labor says My Next Move helps people explore over 900 occupations.[^onet] Use it to understand the work behind a title, then decide which resume evidence belongs near the top.

Example:

**Before tailoring for a product engineering role**

- Built internal admin tools for operations team.
- Fixed bugs across checkout, billing, and onboarding.
- Partnered with design on account settings flow.
- Wrote SQL queries for weekly product metrics.

**After tailoring**

- Partnered with product and design to ship an account settings flow in React and TypeScript.
- Debugged checkout and onboarding issues across billing, forms, and lifecycle emails.
- Wrote weekly SQL queries to track activation and identify setup drop-off.
- Built internal admin tools that reduced manual operations handoffs.

The facts did not change. The reader's path through the facts did.

Checkpoint: the first half of the resume should now contain the strongest role match.

## Step 4: Rewrite bullets with the employer's language and your proof

Use job-description language only where it makes a true bullet clearer.

The safest bullet shape is:

> Action + target skill or context + proof of scope + outcome

MIT recommends action verbs, relevant tasks, job-description language, and outcomes.[^mit] Yale's Office of Career Strategy similarly teaches an Action + Project + Result structure and asks candidates to provide specific examples of impact and value.[^yale]

Here is how that looks in practice.

**Job signal:** "Own customer onboarding improvements."

Weak tailoring:

- Customer onboarding, onboarding, customer success, activation.

Better tailoring:

- Rebuilt customer onboarding checklist and lifecycle email drafts to make setup steps clearer for new accounts.

**Job signal:** "Use data to improve product decisions."

Weak tailoring:

- Data-driven product thinker with analytics mindset.

Better tailoring:

- Wrote weekly SQL activation queries and summarized setup drop-off patterns for product planning.

**Job signal:** "Coordinate cross-functional launches."

Weak tailoring:

- Cross-functional collaborator and stakeholder manager.

Better tailoring:

- Coordinated launch handoffs between design, support, and engineering so customer-facing teams had updated setup notes before release.

If you have exact numbers, use them. If you have a defensible estimate, qualify it. If a number would be fake, use non-numeric proof. The point is to write a bullet you can explain in an interview. For more examples, use the [honest resume bullet protocol](/blog/write-resume-bullets-without-inventing-metrics).

Checkpoint: each edited bullet should include either a target skill, a relevant context, or a clearer outcome. If it only adds a buzzword, undo it.

## Step 5: Keep the resume readable for people and systems

An ATS-friendly tailored resume is still a human-readable resume.

There is a lot of bad advice about applicant tracking systems. The practical version is simple: use clean structure, real text, recognizable section headings, and accurate role language. Do not hide keywords. Do not paste the job description into the bottom of the file. Do not make the resume harder for a person to read in order to satisfy a tool you cannot inspect.

Greenhouse support documentation describes resume parsing as a process that scans an imported resume and fills candidate-profile fields with detected information; it also notes that image-only resumes can cause parsing problems.[^greenhouse] That is enough to support a boring rule: make the resume parseable as text.

Use this checklist:

- Keep standard headings such as Experience, Education, Skills, and Projects.
- Use real text, not a screenshot of a resume.
- Avoid hiding text in white, tiny, or off-page formatting.
- Keep bullets short enough to scan.
- Use the same skill terms the employer uses only where they are accurate.
- Export and review the PDF before sending.

CareerOneStop suggests 5-8 bullets for each job when space allows, one or two lines per bullet, and emphasis on the tasks and accomplishments most relevant to the target job.[^careeronestop] For a one-page resume, you may use fewer. Relevance beats completeness.

Tiny CV helps here because the source stays plain markdown while the preview shows whether the final page still works as a printable document. You can tailor the language without accidentally turning the layout into the main event.

Checkpoint: a human should understand the resume without knowing which keywords you were targeting.

## Step 6: Save a role-specific version

Save the tailored resume as a separate version so you can compare it against the baseline later.

Role-specific versions prevent two problems.

First, they keep you from overwriting your general resume with wording that only makes sense for one job. Second, they make interview prep easier because you can reopen the exact resume you sent and review the evidence behind each bullet.

Use a simple naming convention:

- `baseline`
- `product-engineer`
- `customer-success`
- `data-analyst`
- `founder-operator`

Each version should change emphasis, not truth.

For formal applications, send the PDF the employer asks for. For outreach, warm intros, or follow-ups, a public resume link can be easier to open and forward. The decision framework in [public resume link vs PDF](/blog/public-resume-link-vs-pdf) covers when to use each format.

Tiny CV supports this workflow directly: keep the markdown version close to the facts, publish a clean public CV link when a person needs to read it, and export a PDF when the application system asks for a file.

Checkpoint: you should be able to tell which version went to which role without rereading the whole resume.

## A 30-minute tailoring workflow

Use this timer when you need to tailor one resume without overworking it.

| Time | Task | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0-5 minutes | Mark the job description | 6-10 target signals. |
| 5-10 minutes | Build the proof inventory | Evidence mapped to each signal. |
| 10-18 minutes | Reorder sections and bullets | Strongest match moves higher. |
| 18-25 minutes | Rewrite 4-8 bullets | Job language plus true proof. |
| 25-30 minutes | Scan for honesty and fit | PDF-ready role-specific version. |

Stop after 30 minutes unless the role is unusually important.

Over-tailoring can make the resume worse. If every sentence is rewritten around one posting, the document can start to sound generic because it mirrors the employer more than it explains you.

The right tailored resume gives the reader less work to do. It says, quickly and honestly: this is the role, here is the matching proof, and nothing here was added just to game the system.
